# Redox Titration of Hydrogen Peroxide

## Introduction 

Titrations are some of the most accurate, and most useful, analytical techniques. In this lab you will determine the precise concentration of a store-bought hydrogen peroxide solution via titration. Hydrogen peroxide $${\rm H_2 O_2}$$ is a common household chemical that is useful as an antiseptic and is generally sold as a dilute aqueous solution. Concentrated solutions of hydrogen peroxide are extremely caustic and too hazardous to be available to the general public. One property of hydrogen peroxide is that it is somewhat unstable, decomposing over time to water and . This lab illustrates how one could test the quality of the commercially available product using a redox titration.

For this lab you will be using potassium permanganate $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ as your titrant. You will be supplied with a solution of $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ that is approximately 0.02 M in concentration, which you will use throughout the lab period. Your first task is to standardize this solution -- to determine as accurately as possible the exact concentration of the solution. Potassium permanganate solutions are prepared by dissolving the solid in water; however, due to the high reactivity of $${\rm KMnO_4}$$, the solid is never of analytical purity and it is prone to reaction with impurities in the water used in the preparation of the solution. Therefore, we cannot determine precisely the concentration of the solution by just knowing how much was added. Standardization of reagents is one of the most fundamental tasks in a chemistry lab. Only by verifying and ascertaining exact solution values can we hope to achieve meaningful data.

We will use ferrous ammonium sulfate $${\rm Fe(NH_4)_2 (SO_4)_2 \cdot 6 H_2 O}$$ (commonly known as FAS), to standardize the permanganate solution. This reagent is called a primary standard because it is readily available in very high purity and is convenient to weigh out precisely. To determine the $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ concentration we will add it in a controlled fashion using a burette, a device that allows us to deliver varying and precisely known volumes of a solution, to a known quantity of FAS. The net ionic reaction for the standardization is:

$${\rm MnO_4^-} (aq) + 5 {\rm Fe^{2+}} (aq) + 8 {\rm H_3 O^+} (aq) \rightarrow {\rm Mn^{2+}} (aq) + 5 {\rm Fe^{3+}} (aq) + 12 {\rm H_2 O} (l) \tag{1}$$

This is an example of a redox reaction: the permanganate ion (Mn in the +7 oxidation state) is reduced to $${\rm Mn^{2+}}$$, while the ferrous ion $${\rm Fe^{2+}}$$ is oxidized to the ferric ion $${\rm Fe^{3+}}$$. The reaction must be performed in a fairly acidic solution, as indicated by the presence of hydronium ions $${\rm H_3 O^+}$$ in the equation.

How can we tell that we have added exactly the right amount of $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ to react with all the $${\rm Fe^{2+}} (aq)$$? We rely on the fact that $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ is an intense purple color whereas $${\rm Mn^{2+}} (aq)$$ is a colorless ion. Once all the $${\rm Fe^{2+}} (aq)$$ is consumed, the first excess drop of permanganate solution is sufficient to cause a noticeable color change from colorless to light pink. This color change is called the endpoint of the titration -- the point where you will stop adding titrant. (The $${\rm Fe^{3+}}$$ product is also colorless in the presence of phosphoric acid, which we also add to the flask.) We can measure the volume of permanganate added from the burette, and by knowing the precise mass of FAS in the solution we can determine the precise concentration of the permanganate solution.

A similar procedure can be used to determine the hydrogen peroxide concentration, which also undergoes a redox reaction with permanganate to form $${\rm Mn^{2+}} (aq)$$ and $${\rm O_2} (g)$$:

$$2 {\rm MnO_4^-} (aq) + 5 {\rm H_2 O_2}  (aq) + 6 {\rm H_3 O^+} (aq) \rightarrow 2 {\rm Mn^{2+}} (aq) + 14 {\rm H_2 O} (l) + 5 {\rm O_2} (g) \tag{2}$$

As the reagent is sold as a solution, the best way to assay the concentration is to sample a precisely known volume using a volumetric pipette (*why this piece of glassware?*). Further dilution of the sample is required and this can be achieved precisely also using a volumetric flask. Portions of the diluted solution can then be titrated against the same permanganate solution -- the endpoint of the titration is similar to that observed in the titration with FAS.

### Standardization with FAS titration 

- Obtain and thoroughly clean a 50 mL burette thoroughly with deionized water. Then rinse the burette with a few mL of 4.5 M sulfuric acid $${\rm H_2 SO_4}$$ and finally with a few mL of the 0.02 M $${\rm KMnO_4}$$. After each wash, allow some of the liquid to drain through the stopcock. Needless to say, all of these washes are waste.

- Use a clean glass funnel to fill the burette with the permanganate solution and let a small amount run through the stopcock. Make sure that no air bubbles remain trapped. Make sure that the top of the solution is on scale and note the initial volume. Always remove the funnel before titrating.

- Weigh out by difference[^1], approximately 0.75 g of FAS using a weighing bottle on the analytical balance. Remember, getting exactly 0.7500 g is neither necessary nor appropriate (it wastes too much time). Instead, it is just important that the precise mass used be recorded. See the [this page](lab_equipment#mass-measurements-with-analytical-balances) on details regarding using analytical balances. *Notebook tip: have tables to collect these types of values ready in your notebook before lab begins.*

[^1]: Note: always weigh solids by difference. First, weigh the bottle and cap. Then, outside the balance room, add a quantity of FAS to the bottle. Check the mass and adjust if necessary by adding or removing FAS. Once you have about the correct amount, carefully re-weigh the bottle, cap, and FAS and record the mass. Then, pour the FAS into a clean 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. Finally re-weigh the bottle and cap. The difference in mass between these last two measurements is the mass of FAS added to the flask. Remember, it is not important that you add exactly 0.7500 g of FAS; it is important that you know precisely how much you actually add.

- Dissolve the FAS in about 40 mL of deionized water. Add 10 mL of 4.5 M sulfuric acid and 2 mL of 85% phosphoric acid $${\rm H_3 PO_4}$$ (the phosphoric acid is added to help sharpen the endpoint of the titration). These volumes can be measured with a graduated cylinder; precision is not important for these measurements. *Why?*

- Perform your first titration **very** fast (2-3 minutes at most). This fast, preliminary titration is called a *pilot titration* and is used to get the approximate titrant volume that will be needed. Then, in subsequent titrations, 90% of the titrant volume should be added immediately, which will substantially speed up the process. Pilot titrations are not included in your statistics. *Notebook tip: color changes and solution appearances are important observations to make.*

- Swirling the flask vigorously between additions, titrate the solution carefully by adding permanganate solution from the burette. The purple color will disappear quickly when the flask is swirled. Titrate until a pale pink color persists[^2] in the solution for at least 15 seconds while swirling. This is the endpoint of the titration -- the titration volume should be approximately consistent with your pilot titration results.

[^2]: Putting a sheet of white paper under the flask will help you better observe the endpoint.

- Repeat the titration until you obtain at least two consistent values of $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ molarity. Using your titration results, calculate the concentration of the $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ in $${\rm mol \, L^{−1}}$$ for each consistent trial and report them to your instructor before moving on (you will need the standardized $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ concentration from the combined data of your lab section to complete your analysis!).

### Titrating commercial peroxide solution 

- Using a 10-mL volumetric pipette, add 10.00 mL of store-bought hydrogen peroxide $${\rm H_2 O_2}$$ into a 100 mL volumetric flask. Dilute the solution to the mark with deionized water and shake thoroughly. *Why a pipette? Why not a graduated cylinder?*

- Pipette 10.00 mL of the diluted peroxide solution into a clean beaker and add 25 mL of deionized water and 10 mL of 4.5 M sulfuric acid.

- Titrate in the same manner as in Part 1: until a pale pink color persists for 15 seconds. Don't forget to start with a pilot titration.

- Repeat the titration to get at least two consistent trials. As before, calculate the molar concentration of the *dilute* $${\rm H_2 O_2}$$ for each trial and report the results to your instructor before moving on - they will provide the combined results from your lab section for the rest of the data analysis!

- Make sure that you record the manufacturer's value of the hydrogen peroxide concentration from the bottle before you leave (this is given as a percent by mass). *Notebook tip:* any time you are analyzing a commercial product, it is important to (a) take precise measurements of the whole product, (b) to record all of the information provided by the manufacturer, and (c) never fully trust the values that these companies report, especially if the purpose of your work is to measure that value and assess their claims.

- Use the remaining lab time to begin working on your data analysis.

## Safety and Waste Disposal 

Concentrated solutions of both sulfuric and phosphoric acid are dangerous. Gloves must be worn at all times. If solutions come in contact with skin, wash skin thoroughly with running water for several minutes. Clean up any spills immediately. Any spills larger than 3-5 mL should be neutralized with sodium bicarbonate prior to clean up.

Potassium permanganate is an irritant and will stain skin and clothing. Exercise caution when using potassium permanganate and wash exposed skin thoroughly to avoid irritation. Glassware that has contained $${\rm KMnO_4}$$ and that has been exposed to the air for long periods may be stained with a brown deposit of $${\rm MnO_2}$$. This will not come off by just washing with water. A solution of sulfuric acid containing some hydrogen peroxide can be used to remove these stains and some will be provided for your use. Return burettes clean.

All waste solutions, and first rinses, should be disposed of in the large hazardous waste barrel.
